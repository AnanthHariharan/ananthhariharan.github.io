Room III — The Eclectic Catalog

A house from the catalogue.

The Victorian century did not choose a style. It chose them all, at once, from a printed catalogue, and ordered them shipped by rail. Gothic Revival, Romanesque Revival, Queen Anne, Italianate, Second Empire — five styles in fifty years, all of them ornamented with industrial machinery, all of them serving a customer rather than a patron.

The Victorian permission slip

The reign of Victoria (1837 – 1901) coincides with the industrial revolution, the railway, the catalogue, and the first true middle class. For the first time in history a large population can afford ornament and wants it. The architectural profession, which had spent the previous three centuries deciding between Classical and Gothic, suddenly has neither the moral authority nor the economic incentive to choose for them. Anything that could be made could be sold. Anything that could be sold could be built.

The result was not a style but a condition — eclecticism. A single block of a nineteenth-century city might carry a Gothic Revival church, an Italianate bank, a Second Empire hotel, a row of Queen Anne houses, and a Romanesque Revival armoury, each built within ten years of the others, none of them in formal disagreement with the rest. The mixing was the point.

Industrial ornament

The hidden engine of the Victorian century is the steam-powered scroll saw. Before about 1840, ornament had to be hand-carved by a skilled craftsman; afterwards it could be cut by the metre in a factory, shipped by rail, and nailed up by a competent carpenter. Brackets, finials, spindles, fretwork, bargeboards, turned posts — everything an exuberant house could want — became a line item in a builder's catalogue.

The same revolution happens in cast iron. The great glass-and- iron buildings of the period — the Crystal Palace in London, the original Halles in Paris, the train sheds at every major terminus — depend on prefabricated cast-iron columns and beams that arrived on flatcars and were assembled on site. For the first time, ornament was no longer the bottleneck. The bottleneck was choosing what to order.

The catalogue mind:

  • Pattern books — published collections of plans and elevations a local builder could copy without ever consulting an architect.
  • Mail-order houses — by 1900, Sears and Aladdin would sell complete kits, shipped by rail in numbered crates.
  • Pre-cut mouldings, brackets, gingerbread — all sold by the running foot.
  • Cast-iron façades — entire street-fronts of cast iron, painted to imitate stone, bolted to a brick structure behind.

Gothic Revival — the moral case

The most intellectually serious of the Victorian revivals is the Gothic. Pugin, Ruskin, and the Cambridge Camden Society argued openly that the Gothic was the only honest style for a Christian country — that the Renaissance and the Classical revival had been a pagan interruption, and that good architecture was a moral act. The Houses of Parliament in London, half the churches built between 1840 and 1900, and most of the early Oxbridge collegiate buildings are Gothic Revival on principle.

The American collegiate Gothic — Princeton, Yale, the University of Chicago, the older quadrangles of Notre Dame — was a later import. Its sponsors wanted, in stone, the impression that the school had been there for centuries when in fact it had been there for decades.

Romanesque Revival — Richardson's American invention

The Romanesque Revival as an American phenomenon is largely the work of one architect, Henry Hobson Richardson. Trained at the Beaux-Arts, working in Boston in the 1870s and 1880s, Richardson produced a small handful of buildings — Trinity Church Boston, the Marshall Field Wholesale Store in Chicago, the Allegheny Courthouse in Pittsburgh — that defined a new American mode. The style is muscular: rough-faced stone, broad round arches, short squat towers, deep entry portals, a complete refusal of the spindly Victorian decorative vocabulary.

What Richardson does is unusual — he picks a single historical style and applies it consistently. In a period that was ordering ornament from a catalogue, his buildings argue for discipline. Most of the American libraries, train stations, and courthouses built in his manner between 1885 and 1910 are consciously imitating him.

Queen Anne — picturesque, not historical

The Queen Anne style, despite the name, has very little to do with the actual Queen Anne (reigned 1702 – 1714). It is a late- Victorian invention, English at first, then enthusiastically imported into the United States in the 1880s, which selects the bits of early-eighteenth-century English domestic architecture it likes — tall chimneys, tile-hung gables, asymmetric massing — and combines them with whatever else looks picturesque.

In American hands the Queen Anne becomes the painted lady — the asymmetric, polychrome wooden house with a corner turret, a wraparound porch, decorative spindlework, and a steeply pitched roof broken up by gables in three different directions. The surviving Queen Annes of San Francisco are the most famous in the world, but every American town built in the 1880s and 1890s has them.

Italianate and Second Empire

Two further Victorian modes deserve mention, because they fill out most of what is left.

Italianate arrives in the 1840s as a relaxation of the Greek Revival into something more domestic and more romantic — taller and narrower than its Classical cousins, with a flat or low-pitched roof, deep overhanging eaves supported by paired brackets, tall arched windows, and a decorative belvedere or cupola on top. Most of the brownstone fronts of New York, Boston, and Philadelphia are Italianate.

Second Empire is named for the reign of Napoleon III in France (1852 – 1870), during which Paris was rebuilt by Haussmann in the style of the great Tuileries and Louvre additions. The defining feature is the mansard roof — the steep, double-pitched roof, almost a wall in itself, with dormer windows set into its lower slope. The mansard does two things at once: it adds an entire inhabitable storey above the cornice, and it signals, at a glance, that the building is up to date with Parisian fashion.

Every architectural movement since has had to reckon with the shift the Victorians made — from buildings as patronage to buildings as product.

What the Eclectic building believes

The Victorian century is the first in which architecture is designed for consumers rather than patrons. The catalogue is the operative document. A house becomes an assemblage of off-the-shelf pieces, chosen by taste rather than tradition. The architect's role contracts; the builder's expands; the owner's expands further still, because she is now selecting ornament the way she selects wallpaper.

What the eclectic building believes — to the extent it believes anything — is that history is a shelf rather than a tradition. Gothic, Romanesque, Renaissance, Queen Anne, Italianate are no longer competing theories of the world. They are decorative options. You choose the one that suits your house, your block, your mood, or your budget, and the choice has no further metaphysical implications.

This is a position the Classical and Medieval lineages would both have found incomprehensible. Both believed the styles were arguments. The Victorians, treating them as products, domesticated them in a way no previous century had. Whether that was a degradation or a democratisation is the question the next room — the Craft and Period revivals — was founded to answer.
