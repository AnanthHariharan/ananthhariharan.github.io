Room II — The Medieval Lineage

A wall that climbs.

Romanesque and Gothic. Two centuries of solving a single structural problem — how does stone, the heaviest of materials, stop feeling heavy? — and discovering, in the process, that an architecture could be a theology.

The Romanesque — refuge and the round arch

Romanesque is the in-between. Rome had collapsed, the Gothic had not yet been invented, and Europe was nervously rebuilding in stone after centuries of timber. Romanesque churches — built across the eleventh and twelfth centuries, from Spain to Lombardy to England — look like what you would design if you had heard about Roman architecture from a grandparent: round arches, thick walls, small windows, barrel vaults that needed to be heavy because nobody had quite worked out how to make them light.

The result is enormously beautiful in a way the later styles are not. A Romanesque interior feels like a refuge. The walls are massive because they are doing real work, holding up a heavy stone roof. The windows are small because cutting bigger ones would weaken the wall. Every surface you see is structurally honest. The building is not pretending.

Pull a single round arch out of a Romanesque cloister and you have the entire vocabulary in miniature. Two thick piers, a semicircle of voussoirs spanning between them, a small carved capital where the arch meets the pier. The mathematics is simple — the radius of the arch is half the gap between the piers — but the effect is the foundation of a whole civilisation of building.

Romanesque motifs

  • The round arch — semicircular, always. The single most reliable way to tell Romanesque from Gothic.
  • Thick piers in place of slender columns.
  • Barrel and groin vaults — heavy, half-cylindrical stone ceilings.
  • Blind arcading — rows of arches applied to a wall as ornament rather than openings.
  • Tympanum carvings — sculpted scenes (usually a Last Judgement) in the semicircle above a doorway.
  • Apsidal east end — the semicircular projection that closes the church behind the altar.

The philosophical reading of Romanesque is that it is a style for an age that does not yet trust its own engineering. It compensates with mass. There is something monastic and patient about it — the building is built to last, not to dazzle. Spend an hour in a Romanesque crypt and you understand why so many of the great contemplative orders were founded in the eleventh century.

The Gothic breakthrough — Saint-Denis, 1140

The Gothic begins, as nearly as we can date it, in the choir of the Abbey of Saint-Denis outside Paris, finished in 1144 under the patronage of the Abbot Suger. What Suger and his unnamed master mason did was not a new style so much as a new system — the simultaneous deployment of three structural inventions that, used together, made the Romanesque wall obsolete.

The three inventions

The three are the pointed arch, the rib vault, and the flying buttress. They are useful one at a time. Used together they re-organise European architecture for the next four centuries.

IThe pointed arch

A round arch can only span a width equal to its diameter. To make it taller, you must make it wider. A pointed arch breaks that constraint: the two halves rise to whatever height the builder wants. Pointed arches can be made tall and narrow, and they direct their thrust downward more steeply than the round arch does. This single move is what allows Gothic churches to be twice the height of their Romanesque predecessors on the same footprint.

IIThe rib vault

The Romanesque ceiling was a continuous vault of solid stone. Heavy, hard to build, and resting on the full length of the wall. The Gothic ceiling replaces the vault with a skeleton of stone ribs that take the structural load, with thinner web panels filling in between them. The weight of the roof is now concentrated at the specific points where the ribs meet the walls — and at those points only.

IIIThe flying buttress

The third invention is what makes everything else legible. Outside the building, a sequence of stone half-arches reaches across to the upper wall, catches the outward thrust of the rib vault, and shoves it down into a free-standing pier some distance from the church. The wall, freed of its load-bearing duty, can be cut almost entirely away and replaced with glass.

Light as theology

The Abbot Suger was a Neoplatonist. He believed that light was the closest physical analogue to the divine, and that a church flooded with coloured light from above was, quite literally, a foretaste of heaven. The three structural inventions above are not, in his mind, an engineering triumph but a theological one — they were the means by which a wall could be turned into a window, and a window could be turned into a sermon.

Everything Gothic — the verticality, the dissolution of the wall, the rose windows, the lacy tracery — is in service of that conviction. The Classical building flatters you with your own proportions. The Gothic building does the opposite. It shrinks you on purpose, points up, and floods the dimensions above your head with light you cannot reach. The verticality is the argument. The light is the proof.

The Classical room says you are the measure of the world. The Gothic room says you are not, and points up.

Gothic motifs

  • Pointed arch — the single Gothic giveaway. If the arches are pointed, you are looking at Gothic or a Gothic revival.
  • Rib vault — stone ceiling divided by a skeleton of crossing ribs, usually with a carved keystone at the intersection.
  • Flying buttress — the external half-arch propping the wall from the outside.
  • Tracery — the slender stonework dividing a large window into smaller lights, classified as plate, geometric, curvilinear, or perpendicular by period.
  • Rose window — the great circular window, usually over the west door, often eight to twelve metres across.
  • Crockets and finials — the carved foliate ornaments running up the edges of spires and pinnacles.
  • Gargoyles — functional rainwater spouts shaped as grotesques.
  • The lancet — the tall, narrow, single-light pointed window, usually grouped in threes.

What the Medieval building believes

The Medieval lineage, taken as a whole, holds a position the Classical lineage cannot. It does not believe the world is symmetrical or measurable by human proportions. It believes the world is fallen, that the human body is not the unit of all things, and that the proper response of a building is to gesture beyond itself.

What the Romanesque does by mass, the Gothic does by dematerialisation. The Romanesque crypt protects you from a dangerous world. The Gothic nave admits you, briefly, into a rehearsal of a better one. Each is an act of metaphysical framing — a deliberate decision about what the building is for — and each invents the structural means to perform that framing convincingly.

The objection that the Classical lineage will raise against the Medieval is that this is all in poor taste — that a building that tries to be a sermon is a building that has forgotten how to be a building. The objection the Medieval will raise back is that the Classical, in its dignified calm, has forgotten what a building is for. The quarrel between these two positions runs through almost everything in the next room.
